How to Use This Study Guide

This book is meant to be read, marked up and worked through. Each chapter explains one business model in plain English and then turns it into a plan you can follow, with tools described by category so the advice stays useful as products come and go.

Three ways to read it

If you want the big picture, read the Introduction and Chapter 9, “How to Pick Yours,” first. Together they give you the whole map in under an hour. Then read the chapter for the model that fits you best.

If you’re studying, read the chapters in order. Do the review questions before you check the Answer Key, and fill in each worksheet before moving on. The models build on one another, and later chapters refer back to earlier ones.

If you’re ready to start, go straight to the Workbook at the back. Complete the Advantage Inventory and the Model Scorecard, choose a model, and use Chapter 10’s ninety-day plan alongside your model’s chapter.

A few conventions

  • “Illustrative” on a chart or table means the numbers are realistic teaching examples, not measured data. Use them to understand the shape of an idea, then replace them with your own numbers.
  • Prices and budgets are given in U.S. dollars and reflect typical costs for small, early-stage businesses. Expect them to change.
  • Tools are named by category, such as “an AI assistant subscription,” “a no-code app builder,” or “a payment processor.” The Tool Categories Directory in the back explains what each category does and what to look for when choosing one.
  • Boxes marked Plain English restate a tricky idea simply. Watch Out boxes flag the trap for each model, and Try This boxes give you a small action to take right away.
Try this before Chapter 1

On a blank page, write three answers: (1) something you know better than almost anyone, (2) who already pays for help with that today, and (3) whether you’d rather build something people use or something that works on its own. You’ll come back to these three answers throughout the book.
