Answer Key

Suggested answers to every chapter’s review questions. For short-answer questions, these are model answers; yours may be worded differently and still be correct.

Chapter 1 · Software-Enabled Services

  1. B. Finished professional work, produced with AI systems and a small team.
  2. C. Labor costs fall because one person can serve far more clients.
  3. B. Quietly turning back into an agency with headcount.
  4. Clients judge value against their alternative: what a traditional firm charges and how long it takes. Your low cost becomes margin and growth budget. Pricing at cost leaves money on the table and can signal low quality.
  5. Do I know this work? Is it repetitive? Is it mostly text, documents, or data? Do clients already pay for it? Is it unregulated, or can I partner around the regulation?
  6. A point where a qualified person checks AI-produced work before it reaches the client. It catches errors, protects trust, and is the judgment the client is actually paying for.
  7. Revenue per person. Aim for roughly $300,000–$1,000,000+, versus about $100,000–$150,000 at traditional firms.
  8. Partner with a licensed professional who supervises and signs off, or serve only the unregulated portion of the work.

Chapter 2 · Vertical Agent Harnesses

  1. C. Advertising.
  2. B. Users realize they can get the same result elsewhere, and it loses value.
  3. B. The job with the highest combined score.
  4. Vertical products serve one industry deeply (a report tool for home inspectors); horizontal products serve everyone broadly (a general chat assistant).
  5. It proves the AI can do the job cheaply and quickly, before you spend money on software. If it doesn’t work in a workspace, software won’t fix it.
  6. Any four: proprietary knowledge, integrations, customer memory, encoded workflow, rules and compliance, evaluation sets.
  7. It’s the price of general assistants, so it signals “wrapper” and undervalues the hours the harness saves.
  8. Model 1 sells finished work, Model 2 sells a tool professionals use, and Model 4 sells a method that installs into the customer’s own AI.

Chapter 3 · Headless AI Software

  1. B. The documentation and the output format.
  2. C. A continuously updated database of building-permit rules.
  3. B. Agents parse structured data more reliably.
  4. CLI: type one command, get a result (a vending machine). API: send a formatted request, get data back (a takeout window). MCP: a standard plug any compatible agent can use (a universal port).
  5. Any four: fresh or hidden data, following standards precisely, exact calculations, reading messy files, real-world actions, verifying output, remembering.
  6. Give an agent only your docs and ask it to complete a task. If it struggles, rewrite the docs.
  7. Agent usage can spike unexpectedly. Caps prevent surprise bills and build trust with developers.
  8. Builders with a data source, and builders with one hard, narrow skill.

Chapter 4 · Agent-Native Systems

  1. B. The customer’s own AI agent, following the expert’s method.
  2. C. To let customers watch progress and approve decisions.
  3. B. Owning an email list and community, and supporting multiple platforms.
  4. Skills (instructions and examples), commands (shortcuts that start workflows), sub-agents (specialized delegates), connectors (links to other apps).
  5. Courses must be studied and are often unfinished; a plugin does the work using the method the course would have taught.
  6. Diagnose the situation, plan what to do, then do it. Answers will vary by method.
  7. What the skill does and when the AI should use it.
  8. Give away the diagnose skill to attract users; charge for the plan and do skills, the dashboard, and the community.

Chapter 5 · Taste-Led Niche Products

  1. B. $100,000.
  2. B. Building for everyone instead of the inner circle.
  3. A. Hobbyists tracking things in spreadsheets.
  4. Insider taste and understanding can’t be copied, big companies ignore small groups, and passionate communities are loyal and spread the word.
  5. The one thing a product does better than anything else. Examples vary, such as logging an espresso shot in ten seconds.
  6. Any four: one-time purchases, supporter tiers, sponsors, affiliates, merchandise, events.
  7. A free core grows the community and word of mouth; you charge for “more.”
  8. Any three: shareable moments, community creators, in-person events, authentic content, referrals.

Chapter 6 · Ambient Consumer Agents

  1. B. In the background, available without opening a separate app.
  2. B. Fewer than 40% of groups active at week four.
  3. C. The organizer who was stuck doing the job.
  4. Every action is visible to all group members, each of whom belongs to other groups. Every use is a demo.
  5. Any three: speak only when called or useful; confirm before spending; keep messages short and friendly; remember; be proactive at the right moments; be easy to pause and remove.
  6. They have official, free bot support that works in groups (or, for SMS, universal reach), while other channels restrict bots.
  7. Consent and introduction, minimal data, children’s-privacy compliance, a clear privacy policy, AI providers that don’t train on data, and security.
  8. Examples: a weekly team schedule, family weekend plans, a weekly dinner, chores, a running-club route.

Chapter 7 · Proprietary Data Products

  1. B. Nobody contributes until there’s data, and there’s no data until people contribute.
  2. B. Showing averages only when a group has enough contributors.
  3. B. Building a dataset nobody needs.
  4. People use the app → unique data accumulates → the product improves → more people join, and the loop repeats.
  5. Answers a money question; hard to get elsewhere; fresh; granular; trustworthy; legally clean.
  6. Contributors share their own data to unlock everyone else’s. Examples vary.
  7. Dense data in one area is accurate and useful; thin data spread everywhere is neither, and small counts can identify people.
  8. Any four: premium insights, reports, dashboards, API access, an MCP server, bulk licensing.

Chapter 8 · Open-Source-Core Products

  1. B. The hosted cloud version.
  2. C. AGPLv3.
  3. B. Giving everything away with no reason to pay.
  4. Customers can inspect the code and can always self-host if the company disappears, so there’s no lock-in fear.
  5. Hosting, paid tiers, support, community.
  6. What it is and who it’s for, a screenshot, a two-minute quick start, one-command install, the license, and a link to the hosted version.
  7. It destroys community trust. Add paid value on top instead.
  8. “Like [expensive product], but open source and cheaper.” It’s instantly understood and targets known pain around price and lock-in.

Chapter 9 · How to Pick Yours

  1. A. Model 1.
  2. B. Right.
  3. B. Three or more people say they’d pay, or pay a deposit.
  4. It redirects money already being spent, so you don’t have to create a new habit or budget.
  5. Models 1 and 4. They sell expertise you already have and need little building, so you can charge almost immediately.
  6. Having the advantage; handling the technical lift; fitting your income timeline; wanting to do the work every day.
  7. Answers are personal.
