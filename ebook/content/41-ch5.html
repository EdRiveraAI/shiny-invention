5

Taste-Led Niche Products

Everyone has the same tools. Nobody has your obsession.
Learning objectives
  • Explain why taste becomes the scarce ingredient when building is cheap
  • Apply the “1,000 true fans” math to a niche you know
  • Choose a passion you’re genuinely part of and find its unmet needs
  • Build a first version around one core action, with insider taste
  • Earn money gently and grow by word of mouth

Today, AI lets almost anyone build an app, a website, or a digital product in days. When everyone has the same powerful tools, the tools stop being an advantage. What’s still rare is taste: deep knowledge of and love for a specific thing, plus the instinct for what the people who share that love want.

A taste-led niche product is built for a passion community you’re genuinely part of. It doesn’t try to please the whole world. It delights a small group of obsessed people so much that they pay for it and recruit their friends.

Key terms
Taste
An insider’s sense of what matters, what feels right, and what’s missing in a community’s world.
True fan
Someone who will use, pay for, and promote what you make.
Core action
The one thing your product does better than anything else, such as logging a film or a ride.
Affiliate commission
A percentage of a sale paid to you for referring a customer to another seller’s product.
Word of mouth
Growth that comes from users recommending you, not from paid advertising.

The Model Explained

The three circles

CircleWhat it means
EveryoneThe general public. They don’t care about your niche, and you shouldn’t build for them.
Your communityEveryone who shares the passion: all roller-coaster fans, film lovers, or fly-fishers. Thousands to millions of people.
1,000 true fansThe obsessives who’ll use your product daily, pay for it, give feedback, and tell everyone else. Build for this circle first.

The phrase “1,000 true fans” comes from a widely cited 2008 essay by the writer Kevin Kelly. A creator doesn’t need millions of customers to make a good living, only about a thousand people who love the work enough to spend around $100 a year on it. That’s $100,000 a year. With AI, one person can now build and run the whole product, so the math works for a solo founder.

The eight facts, unpacked

Why now. When anyone can build, caring more than anyone else becomes the scarce thing. A big company can copy features, but it can’t copy your understanding of the community.

Real-world example. A social app where film lovers log, rate, and review every movie they watch was built by film obsessives for film obsessives. It started tiny and grew to millions of users, even though large movie databases already had more data. They didn’t have the taste.

Proof it works. The framework’s originator built a roller-coaster app that began at a major theme park: a fan built what coaster fans wanted, such as logging rides, tracking ride counts, and sharing with friends.

Who builds it. Anyone with a passion and a community to build for. You don’t need to be a developer or a business expert. You need to be a genuine insider.

How it earns. A low-cost subscription ($3–$10 a month or $30–$60 a year), purchases (one-time unlocks, digital packs, merchandise), sponsors (brands in the niche paying to reach your audience), and affiliates (commissions when users buy gear you recommend).

The trap. Building for everyone. A generic product gets drowned out. A product built for the thousand who’ll tell their friends spreads by word of mouth, for free.

On the map, this model sits in the lower left. It’s among the least technical models and usually a lifestyle-scale business, often $50,000 to $500,000 a year rather than a venture-backed giant, though occasionally one becomes a household name.

Why This Is a Real Opportunity

  • Big companies ignore small passionate groups. No technology giant will build a tool for competitive birdwatchers, but birdwatchers will gladly pay for one.
  • Building is nearly free. AI app builders let a non-programmer launch a polished app in weeks.
  • Passion communities are loyal. Hobbyists stay for years and promote what they love.
  • Marketing is built in. You already know where the community gathers: its forums, chat servers, conventions, and creators.
  • It’s sustainable for you. You’ll keep working on it because you care.

A pattern that works

“A social diary for ___.” Log it, rate it, collect it, share it with friends. People in hobbies love tracking, collecting, ranking, and showing off.

Case study“Dialed” for home espresso obsessives

The inner circle: people with $1,000-plus home espresso setups who weigh every shot and swap advice in online espresso forums.

Their pain: dialing in each new bag of beans (grind, dose, time, taste) is trial and error, and their notes are scattered across phone notes and spreadsheets.

The product: log each shot in ten seconds (beans, grind setting, dose, yield, time, taste); an AI dial-in coach (“Sour and fast? Grind two steps finer.”); a bean library with roaster details; recipe sharing with friends; and stats (“You’ve pulled 412 shots this year”).

The money: a free basic log; Pro at $4.99 a month or $39 a year (AI coach, unlimited beans, stats); affiliate links to grinders, scales, and roasters; a sponsored “Roaster of the Month.”

The math: 1,500 Pro members × $39 = $58,500 a year, before affiliate and sponsor income.

Phase 0: Pick your passion

  1. List three things you could talk about for hours.
  2. Check that you’re genuinely part of each one: you do it regularly, you know the insider language, the debates, and the well-known people, and you’re active in its online spaces.
  3. Check that the community is reachable and spends money: active forums or chat servers with thousands of members; events, conventions, video creators, and podcasts; people who already buy gear, tickets, or apps.
  4. Pick one, the one you’ll still care about in three years, not the one that looks most profitable.

Research tools: forum-size statistics sites, video platforms, chat-server directories, social-network groups, and search-trend tools to see whether interest is growing.

Phase 1: Listen to the inner circle

  1. Lurk and collect complaints. Read a year of top posts. Note every “I wish there was…,” “Does anyone know an app that…,” and “I track this in a spreadsheet…”
  2. Look at what exists. Read the one- to three-star reviews of existing apps in the app stores.
  3. Talk to ten to twenty true fans. “I’m building something for [hobby] people. Can I ask you five questions?” Ask what they track, what frustrates them, and what they’d love that doesn’t exist.
  4. Pick one core action your product will do better than anything else.
Try this

Spreadsheets are a gold mine. When hobbyists track something in a spreadsheet, they’re asking for an app. Paste a few hundred forum posts or app reviews into an AI assistant and ask it to group the complaints into themes and rank them by frequency.

Phase 2: Build the first version fast

Keep it small: one core action, done beautifully, with your community’s taste built in. Use their language, their inside jokes, and their aesthetic.

The taste checklist (this is your edge)
  • Does it feel made by one of us, in its language, references, and design?
  • Does it include the little details only insiders care about?
  • Is the core action fast and satisfying?
  • Is there something to share, such as stats, collections, badges, or a year in review? Sharing drives word of mouth.

Phase 3: Launch to the inner circle

  1. Run a private beta with 50–100 true fans from your interviews. Make them feel like co-creators.
  2. Create a home for the community; a group chat server is ideal for hobbies.
  3. Ship improvements every week based on feedback, and announce each one. Visible responsiveness builds loyalty.
  4. Start an email list on day one. It’s your owned audience, so you don’t depend on app stores or social algorithms.

Phase 4: Make money, gently

Hobbyists hate being squeezed but gladly support things they love.

Pricing rule: keep the core experience free so the community grows. Charge for “more”: deeper stats, AI features, unlimited items, early access, and status.

Phase 5: Grow by word of mouth

  1. Shareable moments: year-in-review cards, milestone badges, and collection images designed to look great when posted.
  2. Community creators: give niche video makers and podcasters free Pro accounts. Their mentions reach the whole community.
  3. Show up in person at conventions, meetups, tournaments, and parks. Stickers and QR codes at real events work very well.
  4. Authentic content showing you as a member of the community using the product. Authentic beats polished.
  5. Referrals: “Invite three friends, get a month of Pro free.”
  6. Stay small on purpose. Expand to the wider community only once the inner circle loves it. Never water it down for everyone.

Risks and How to Handle Them

RiskMitigation
Building for everyone (the trap)Ask of every feature: “Would my thousand true fans love this?” Say no to the rest.
Niche too small to earnCheck the math early (fans × realistic price). Add affiliates, sponsors, and merchandise.
Burnout from community demandsA public roadmap, a predictable release rhythm, and volunteer moderators from among superfans.
Data or intellectual-property issuesDon’t use copyrighted data, logos, or trademarks without permission. Mentioning a place by name is generally fine; using its logo isn’t. Check API terms. Community-contributed data is safest.
Losing the passionPick something you’ll love for years.
App store rules and feesStores take roughly 15–30%. Offer web sign-up where allowed and use subscription-management tools.

Chapter summary
  • When building is cheap, taste and insider understanding are the scarce ingredients.
  • A thousand true fans spending about $100 a year can sustain a solo business.
  • Pick a passion you’re genuinely part of; mine forums, reviews, and spreadsheets for unmet needs.
  • Build one core action beautifully, with something worth sharing.
  • Keep the core free, charge for “more,” and grow outward from the inner circle by word of mouth.
