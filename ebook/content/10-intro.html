The New Map of AI Business

When anyone can build, the advantage shifts to what you know, who you serve, and what you own.
In this introduction
  • Why the rules for starting a business changed when AI got cheap and capable
  • The eight business models this book covers, in one table
  • How to read the positioning map that appears on every chapter opener
  • The single question that decides which model fits you

For most of the last twenty years, building a software business meant hiring engineers, raising money, designing an app, and then spending a fortune convincing strangers to download it. Running a service business meant the opposite problem: every new client required another person on payroll. Both paths were slow, expensive, and closed to most people.

That has changed, and quickly. AI models can now read, write, summarize, analyze, plan, and take actions in other software. AI coding assistants let people with no programming background build working applications. AI agents, meaning programs that pursue a goal by deciding on steps and using tools without a person clicking every button, are becoming everyday users of software. The cost of producing code, content, and routine professional work has fallen sharply.

When something becomes cheap, value moves to whatever is still scarce. This book is about where it moved, and about the eight distinct ways ordinary people, not just engineers or venture-backed founders, can build a business on that shift.

What Became Cheap, and What Stayed Scarce

Every model in this book rests on one observation: the things that used to be the hard part of building a business are now easy, and the things that used to be taken for granted are now the advantage.

Table I.1 • The great re-pricing

Writing code and building appsKnowing exactly what a niche needs
Drafting documents, reports, and contentJudgment about what’s correct, safe, and good

Read the right-hand column again. None of it requires a computer science degree. It requires experience, relationships, a community, a method, or a passion. That’s why this moment favors people who know a field deeply over people who only know technology.

The model is the engine. Your knowledge, data, community and method are what turn it into a business.

How to Read the Map

Every chapter opener shows a small square with eight dots, one of them highlighted. That’s the map, and it places each model according to two questions.

The horizontal axis: who does the work? On the left, a person does the work, with AI as a helper, and the product is something people look at and interact with. On the right, an AI agent does the work on its own, running in a loop: it acts, checks the result, and acts again while people supervise or aren’t involved at all. Left-side businesses succeed through user experience and relationships. Right-side businesses succeed through reliability, documentation, and scale.

The vertical axis: what are you really selling? Models near the top sell expertise and outcomes directly: finished work, a professional’s workflow, a coach’s method. Models near the bottom build assets that grow in value over time, such as a community of fans, a body of unique data, or widely adopted code. Neither half is better. Top-half models usually earn sooner, and bottom-half models are usually harder to copy once established.

Plain English

Left means people use it; right means agents do it. Top means you sell what you know; bottom means you build something that keeps growing in value.

The One Question That Matters Most

The rest of this book can be boiled down to a single question, which Chapter 9 turns into a full decision process: What do you know better than almost anyone, and who already pays for it?

Your answer points to your model. If people pay firms or freelancers for what you know, start with Model 1. If professionals pay for tools, look at Models 2 through 4. If fans and groups spend money on your passion, look at Models 5 and 6. If businesses buy the kind of information you can gather, look at Model 7. And if you enjoy building openly with a community of developers, look at Model 8.

You won’t pick wrong by reading all eight. Many successful businesses combine two or three of them, starting with one that earns money quickly and growing into one that’s harder to copy. Chapter 10 shows how to stack them.

Key takeaways
  • AI made code, content, and routine work cheap. Value moved to knowledge, judgment, data, trust, community, and taste.
  • There are eight distinct ways to build on that shift. Each favors a different kind of existing advantage.
  • The map sorts the models by who does the work (person or agent) and what is sold (expertise or compounding assets).
  • Your best starting model is the one that matches what you already know and who already pays for it.
