Vertical Agent Harnesses

The model is the engine. The harness is the car built for one road.
Learning objectives
  • Define a vertical agent harness and its five components
  • Tell the difference between a true harness and a thin wrapper
  • Find the most painful job in a niche with the “ten weekly jobs” exercise
  • Prototype a harness without code, then turn it into software
  • Build a moat that grows stronger as AI models improve

Today’s AI models, such as the ones behind popular chat assistants, are engines: enormously powerful and completely general. But an engine on its own doesn’t get anyone to work. You need a car around it, with steering, seats, brakes, and a map. This chapter is about building that car for one specific road.

A vertical agent harness is software that wraps an AI model in everything it needs to do one niche’s job extremely well, so well that a professional in that niche will pay for it instead of using a general chat assistant. “Vertical” means focused on a single industry or type of user. Its opposite, “horizontal,” means built for everyone, like a general-purpose assistant.

Key terms
Vertical vs. horizontal
Vertical products serve one industry deeply; horizontal products serve everyone broadly.
Harness
The tools, knowledge, rules, memory, and workflows built around an AI model for a specific job.
Thin wrapper
A product that is little more than a prompt and a nice screen on top of a general model, and is easily replaced by the next model release.
Moat
Anything that makes a business hard for competitors to copy.
Evaluation set (evals)
A library of real test cases with known-good answers, used to measure whether the product’s output is correct.
Retrieval
Searching a knowledge base for the most relevant documents and giving them to the model as context.

The caption under the diagram, “the harness, built for one niche,” is the key phrase. A general model knows a little about everything. A harness knows everything about one thing, and it plugs into how that niche already works.

The eight facts, unpacked

Why now. Models are now smart enough for almost any reasoning task. What they lack is context (niche knowledge and the user’s own data) and workflow (knowing the exact steps of a job). That gap is the opportunity.

Real-world examples. Some of the fastest-growing AI companies are harnesses: an AI platform for law firms, an AI scribe that listens to doctor–patient conversations and writes the clinical note, and a service that drafts demand letters for personal-injury attorneys and charges per letter. All three use models that anyone can access. Their value is the harness.

Proof it works. One builder created a harness just for people who run tabletop fantasy role-playing games. It shows that the niche can be a hobby, and can be small, as long as users are passionate and the workflow is specific.

Who builds it. Deep experts in a niche the big players will ignore. Large technology companies won’t build a tool for funeral directors, HVAC estimators, or game masters. You can, if you know that world from the inside.

How it earns. A premium subscription, for example $99 to $500 per user per month, which is far more than a general assistant costs because it saves hours. Or per-job pricing: a fixed fee per report, letter, or claim.

The trap. A thin wrapper is a product that’s just a pleasant screen over a general model with a clever prompt. When the next, better model comes out, users realize they can get the same result from a general assistant, and the product dies. A real harness gets better when models improve, because its tools, knowledge, memory, and workflows are where the value lives.

How a harness differs from Models 1 and 4

In Model 1 you do the work for clients and sell results. In Model 2 you sell the tool so professionals do the work faster themselves. In Model 4 (Chapter 4) you sell your method as a package that installs into the customer’s own AI assistant. The three combine well. Many founders start with Model 1, refine their internal system on real clients, then sell that system as a harness.

Why This Is a Real Opportunity

  • Software margins. Once built, each new subscriber costs only AI usage and hosting, so gross margins of 70 to 85 percent are common.
  • Niche means little competition. A niche with 50,000 professionals paying $150 a month is a $90 million-a-year market: too small for the giants, plenty for you.
  • Experts have the edge. AI coding assistants and no-code app builders let a domain expert build working software. The hard part, knowing exactly what the niche needs, is what you already have.
  • Professionals pay for time saved. If a tool saves a $100-an-hour professional five hours a week, $200 a month is an easy yes.

Case studyA harness for home inspectors

The most painful weekly job: writing the inspection report. It takes two to four hours per house, usually at night after the inspection.

PartWhat you’d build
ToolsUpload photos and voice notes from a phone; export into the inspection-report software inspectors already use; email the report to the agent and buyer.
KnowledgeA library of 1,000+ common defects with standard descriptions, repair cost ranges, and reference photos; building-code references.
RulesThe state’s standards of practice; liability-safe phrasing (“recommend evaluation by a licensed electrician”); never state anything that wasn’t observed.
MemoryEach inspector’s writing style, favorite phrases, company template, past reports, and regular real-estate agents.
WorkflowsWalk the house → photograph and dictate → AI identifies each defect and drafts its description → inspector approves or edits → report generated in their template → sent.

The result: report time falls from about three hours to about thirty minutes. At $149 a month, it pays for itself on the first house. A general assistant can’t do this, because it doesn’t know the state rules, the defect library, the inspector’s template, or how to connect to their software.

Phase 0: Choose your niche

  1. You know it deeply: you’ve worked in it, run it, or obsessed over it.
  2. Its people are reachable: associations, online groups, forums, trade shows, newsletters.
  3. They already pay for tools, even small ones.
  4. The work is heavy on text, documents, images, speech, or data.
  5. Big players ignore it. If a giant is already building it, move on.
  6. There’s niche-specific knowledge or rules that general AI gets wrong. That’s your moat.

Niche ideas to spark thinking: home inspectors, insurance adjusters, property managers, homeowners-association managers, funeral homes, veterinary clinics, dental office managers, appraisers, nonprofit grant writers, construction estimators, HVAC and plumbing contractors (quotes and permits), customs brokers, court reporters, church administrators, youth sports league organizers, wedding planners, tabletop gamers, online craft sellers, immigration paralegals, and freight brokers.

Phase 1: The “ten weekly jobs” exercise

List ten jobs your niche does every week. Score each from 1 to 5 on five dimensions, then add up the scores.

Pick the highest score. That single job is your first product. Don’t build for all ten. Then validate it: talk to ten to fifteen people in the niche. Ask how they do it today, how long it takes, what they’ve tried, and what they’d pay to cut it by 80 percent. Don’t pitch. Listen, and record and summarize each call with an AI meeting recorder.

Phase 2: Build the harness by hand first

Before writing any software, prove the AI can do the job:

  1. Gather ten to twenty real examples of the finished work, with permission and anonymized.
  2. In an AI assistant’s project or workspace feature, load the knowledge (reference documents, examples, standards), the rules (a written list of requirements and prohibitions), and the workflow (step-by-step instructions).
  3. Run real jobs through it. Compare the output to expert-quality work and adjust the instructions until at least 80 percent of the output is usable with light edits.
  4. Do the work for a few people for free. They send inputs, you run them through your setup and send results back. You’re temporarily running Model 1, and it teaches you what really matters.
Plain English

Your first harness is a folder of documents and instructions inside an AI assistant. If you can’t make it work there, software won’t fix it. If you can, software just makes it faster and easier to use.

Phase 3: Turn it into software

Route A, no-code (non-technical founders): describe the app to an AI app builder that generates working web apps from plain-language descriptions; use an AI coding assistant to make changes; use a hosted database-and-login service for user accounts and data (often built into app builders); and use a workflow automation tool for background steps.

Route B, developer stack (more control, scales further):

Build only the one workflow. It should take raw input, run your tools, knowledge, rules, memory, and workflow, and produce output that’s 80 percent done. Give users an easy edit screen, and export into the format or software they already use. The export step matters more than most founders expect.

Phase 4: Build your moat

Test your product with one question: if a model twice as smart came out tomorrow, would customers still need us? You want the answer to be yes, for these reasons:

  • Proprietary knowledge: a defect library, clause bank, rate table, or rules database you curated.
  • Integrations with the niche’s specific software.
  • Customer memory: months of each user’s data, style, and history. Switching means losing it.
  • Encoded workflow: the niche’s exact steps, checklists, and formats.
  • Rules and compliance: regulations, liability-safe language, and audit trails.
  • Evaluation sets: a growing library of real test cases proving your output is right.

A smarter model makes every item on this list more valuable. That’s the difference between a harness and a wrapper.

Phase 5: Price and launch

Pricing. For a subscription, price against the time saved; common niche ranges are $49 to $500 per user per month, with a founding-member discount for the first 20 to 50 users. Use per-job pricing when jobs are large and irregular, or combine a base subscription with usage. Avoid $9 to $20 a month. That’s the price of a general assistant, and it signals “wrapper.”

Launch channels. Start with your interview list. Then go to niche communities and forums, industry associations and conferences (a booth or speaking slot can fill a pipeline), and sixty-second demo videos showing a three-hour job done in twenty minutes. Integration marketplaces of software the niche already uses are another channel, along with targeted outbound email built from B2B contact databases. Your landing page should have one problem, one demo, one price, and a free trial.

Phase 6: Grow

Add job #2 from your list, then #3. Each one deepens the harness and supports a higher price. Track weekly active usage, time saved per job, and monthly churn (aim for under 3 percent). Save every user edit, because users’ corrections show you exactly how to improve your knowledge and rules. Consider a done-for-you tier (Model 1) for customers who want you to run it. A few hundred loyal customers make a sellable, fundable business.

Risks and How to Handle Them

RiskMitigation
Becoming a thin wrapperRun the “better model” test every quarter. Invest in knowledge, integrations, and memory.
AI errors in high-stakes fieldsHuman approval before anything is final, citations and sources shown, clear terms of service. Pick lower-stakes niches for a first product.
Data privacy and complianceUse model APIs that don’t train on customer data. Healthcare requires health-privacy compliance and a business associate agreement with every vendor that touches patient data. Pursue a security certification when larger customers ask.
AI usage costs eating marginPrice with usage in mind, cache repeated content, and route simple steps to smaller, cheaper models.
Niche too small or unreachableValidate with 10–15 interviews and confirm communities exist before building.
An incumbent copies youMove faster, go deeper into the niche, and own the community relationships.
