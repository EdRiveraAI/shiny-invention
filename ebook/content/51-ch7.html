7

Proprietary Data Products

Anyone can copy your app. Nobody can copy your data.
Learning objectives
  • Explain the data flywheel and why data is a durable moat
  • Judge whether a dataset is valuable, using six qualities
  • Solve the cold-start problem with proven collection strategies
  • Collect data legally and ethically, with consent and anonymization
  • Package one dataset into several products for different buyers

With AI, anyone can rebuild your app’s features in a weekend. What they can’t rebuild is the unique information your users have given you over months or years: their activities, prices, experiences, reviews, and results. That body of data is proprietary, meaning only you have it, and it can become your most valuable and most defensible asset.

In a proprietary data product, you build a useful app whose real business is the unique data it collects, then sell insights from that data to people and AI agents who need answers nobody else can provide.

Key terms
Proprietary data
Information only you possess, gathered through your own product or relationships.
Data flywheel
A self-reinforcing loop in which usage creates data, data improves the product, and a better product attracts more users.
Cold start
The early problem that no one contributes until there’s data, and there’s no data until people contribute.
Give-to-get
Users contribute their own data to unlock everyone else’s.
Aggregation and anonymization
Combining data into group statistics and removing identifying details, so no individual can be singled out.
Re-identification
Working out who a person is from supposedly anonymous data.

The Model Explained

The data flywheel

A flywheel is a heavy wheel that’s hard to start spinning but, once moving, keeps itself going and speeds up. The data flywheel works the same way:

  1. People use the app because it’s useful to them: logging runs, rating rides, reporting prices.
  2. Unique data accumulates as a side effect.
  3. The product gets better: smarter recommendations, accurate averages, live maps.
  4. More people join, because the product now beats the alternatives.
  5. Back to step 1, with even more data, and the cycle accelerates.

The side arrow, Unique data → “Buyers and agents pay to query it,” shows a second revenue path. Separately from the app, businesses, researchers, and increasingly AI agents pay to ask questions of your data, usually through an API. This connects directly to Model 3: your data can be the source behind a headless tool.

The eight facts, unpacked

Why now. Models are trained on the public internet, so they know nothing about what happened last week in your niche or about things nobody ever wrote down. Whoever owns that data holds what AI can’t generate.

Real-world examples. A fitness-tracking app turned millions of logged runs and rides into heatmaps, and sells aggregated data to city planners designing bike lanes. A crowd-sourced navigation app collected live traffic speeds simply because drivers used it, and that data made it worth about a billion dollars to an acquirer. An anonymous salary-sharing site built a salary database no employer would publish by asking people to share theirs in order to see others’.

Proof it works. The framework’s originator admits they haven’t built one yet. Their idea is that ride logs from their coaster app could become a dataset of popular rides, real wait times, closures, and ratings by park. Most data products start exactly this way, as a side effect of a useful app.

Who builds it. Builders inside a community whose data nobody captures, where important information lives only in people’s heads, group chats, or spreadsheets.

How it earns. A free app maximizes contributors. Revenue comes from paid insights inside the app, reports sold to businesses, and API access for businesses and agents.

The trap. Two traps, in fact. (1) Collecting without consent is unethical, often illegal, and destroys trust. (2) A dataset nobody needs is worthless, no matter how large it is.

Start from a valuable question, then design the collection. Never the other way around.

How it connects to the other models

Data often sits underneath other models. A taste-led app (Model 5) collects it; a headless API (Model 3) sells access to it; it makes a vertical harness (Model 2) smarter than its rivals; and a service business (Model 1) generates it as a by-product, such as real prices from thousands of jobs.

Case study“FairQuote” for home-improvement prices

The question nobody can answer: what does a roof replacement really cost in Tampa? Online estimates are vague ranges, and contractors don’t publish prices.

How to collect it (give-to-get): homeowners upload a contractor quote or invoice to see what others paid for the same job nearby. AI reads the document and extracts job type, size, materials, price, zip code, and date. Personal details are stripped out automatically.

The flywheel: more quotes make local prices more accurate, so more homeowners check prices before hiring, so more quotes come in.

The money: free basic ranges for contributors; a $9.99 “Fair Price Report” for one job in one zip code; a contractor subscription at $49–$199 a month (“How do my prices compare?”); API and licensing deals with insurers, lenders, real-estate platforms, and AI home assistants; and an annual “State of Home Prices” report that earns press coverage.

The moat: after two years and 50,000 verified quotes, no competitor can catch up quickly.

Phase 0: Find the valuable question

  1. List ten questions people in your niche ask that have no good answer: “Does anyone know how much…,” “Is it normal that…,” “What’s the best time to…”
  2. For each, ask: who makes a money decision with this answer? Would businesses pay? Can I collect it legally, with consent? Can people contribute in under sixty seconds? Does it need to be fresh? (Fresh is better, because it keeps you needed.)
  3. Pick one question with at least three types of buyers.
  4. Validate with buyers early. Ask five businesses: “If I had verified data on X by zip code, would you pay? How much? In what format?”

Make contributing effortless: a photo upload with AI extraction, one-tap logging, or automatic capture. Every extra field cuts contributions. Tools by category: AI app builders or simple online forms to start; AI vision models or specialized receipt- and document-reading services for extraction; location services (with consent); public open-data portals and, where permitted, web-data extraction services for seeding; digital gift-card platforms for rewards; and survey panels to seed early responses.

Phase 2: Consent, privacy, and legal

This is the first half of the trap, so treat it as non-negotiable.

  1. Plain-language consent: tell contributors exactly what you collect, how it’s used, and that aggregated, anonymized insights may be sold. No hidden fine print.
  2. Anonymize and aggregate: sell patterns, not people. Strip identifiers, and never show results based on just one or two data points. A common rule is to publish averages only when a group has five to ten or more contributors.
  3. Watch for re-identification. One fitness app’s public heatmap once revealed the locations of secret military facilities. Location data needs special care: blur start and end points and suppress sparse areas.
  4. Know the laws. In the U.S., California’s privacy law and a growing list of state laws give people rights, including opting out of the “sale” of their data; health data carries extra rules (HIPAA and state health-data laws). In the EU and UK, GDPR requires a lawful basis such as explicit consent, and a right to deletion. For children, COPPA applies in the U.S., so avoid collecting from children.
  5. Have a lawyer review your terms and privacy policy before launch. It’s worth a few hundred to a couple of thousand dollars.

Tool categories: privacy-policy and consent-banner generators; privacy-request management platforms as you grow; and open-source differential-privacy libraries for advanced, privacy-preserving statistics.

Phase 3: Store, clean, and verify

Raw data is messy, and clean, trustworthy data is what people pay for. Use a hosted relational database to start and a cloud data warehouse as you grow; data-transformation tools to clean and model; data-pipeline tools to pull from multiple sources; AI to standardize categories (“roof replacement” = “re-roof” = “new roof”), extract fields, and flag oddities; automated data-quality testing; fraud and duplicate detection (duplicate receipts, impossible values, suspicious bursts); and notebook or BI tools to explore. Trust signals to build in: “verified by receipt” badges, a sample size beside every number (“based on 47 quotes”), last-updated dates, and outlier removal.

“Agents pay to query it” is the newest and fastest-growing buyer. When someone asks their AI, “Is $14,000 a fair price for a roof in Tampa?”, the agent could call your API for the answer. Make your data agent-friendly: clear docs, JSON output, and an MCP server.

Phase 5: Grow the flywheel

  1. Seed the first 500–1,000 data points yourself with rewards, partners, public data, and your community.
  2. Launch in one area first (one city, one park, one category) so the data is dense and useful, then expand. Thin data everywhere is useless.
  3. Publish free insights as marketing: “The real cost of a roof in Florida, 2026” earns press, search traffic, and contributors. Use chart-publishing tools for shareable graphics.
  4. Close the loop: show contributors their impact (“Your quote helped 212 neighbors”).
  5. Approach business buyers once coverage is real, with sample reports as the hook.
  6. Track flywheel metrics: contributions per week, returning contributors, coverage, and revenue per data point.

Chapter summary
  • Code and models are commodities; fresh, niche, proprietary data is scarce and hard to copy.
  • The data flywheel (use → data → better product → more users) accelerates over time.
  • Valuable data answers money questions, is hard to get, fresh, granular, trustworthy, and legally clean.
  • Solve the cold start with single-player value, give-to-get, seeding, and one dense launch area.
  • Consent and anonymization come first; then package the same data as reports, dashboards, APIs, and licenses.
