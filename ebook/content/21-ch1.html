Software-Enabled Services

Don’t sell professionals software. Become the firm that’s ten times faster.
Learning objectives
  • Explain why selling finished work can beat selling software
  • Calculate how AI changes the economics of a service firm
  • Choose a niche using five screening questions
  • Map a deliverable, automate 80% of it, and keep quality high
  • Price, launch, and scale without turning back into an agency

Many people who see what AI can do decide to build software and sell it to accountants, lawyers, marketers, or recruiters. It’s a hard road. Professionals are slow to adopt new tools, sales cycles are long, and hundreds of funded startups are already competing for the same customers. This chapter describes the opposite approach: skip the software sale and sell the finished work.

The client doesn’t want a bookkeeping tool. They want their books closed. They don’t want contract-review software. They want to know whether the contract is safe to sign. A software-enabled service is a business that sells that result, like any traditional firm, but produces it with a very small team running on AI systems. From the outside it looks like a normal professional firm. Inside, it runs like a technology company.

Key terms
Software-enabled service
A business that sells finished professional work (not software) and uses AI systems internally to produce it with far fewer people.
Deliverable
The specific thing a client receives: closed books, a reviewed contract, a monthly report, a set of articles.
Retainer
A recurring monthly fee for an agreed scope of ongoing work.
Per-outcome pricing
Charging for a result, such as a hire made or a claim resolved, rather than for time spent.
Human-in-the-loop review
A checkpoint where a qualified person checks AI-produced work before it reaches the client.
Revenue per person
Total revenue divided by the number of people on the team. It’s the key health metric for this model.

The Model Explained

Reading the diagram

The hero diagram in Figure 1.1 has three parts. On the left, Before: a traditional firm needs roughly twenty people to serve its clients. On the right, After: an AI-native firm delivers the same work with two people supported by AI systems. Both arrows point to the same box, Same client outcome. That’s the whole idea. The client experience stays the same, or gets better because work arrives faster, while the cost of producing it collapses.

The eight facts, unpacked

Why now. Clients have always wanted the job done rather than a tool to learn. Until recently, doing the job meant hiring people, because only people could read documents, make judgment calls, draft, and check. AI can now do most of the repetitive portion of that work: categorizing, extracting, summarizing, drafting, formatting, and checking against a list. A small team can now deliver at a scale that once required a floor of staff.

Real-world examples. Firms built this way already exist. One kind of AI-native law practice focuses narrowly on reviewing contracts quickly. Another kind of firm handles bookkeeping for startups using its own software plus a small accounting team. Both look like service firms to their customers and run like software companies inside.

Who builds it. Anyone who has run or worked in a service business. Your advantage isn’t technical skill. It’s knowing the work well enough to see which 80% is repetitive and which 20% needs judgment.

How it earns. Like a service business: monthly retainers, per-project fees, or per-outcome pricing. You aren’t charging software subscriptions, which means you can charge what the outcome is worth to the client.

The trap. When demand grows, the instinct is to hire people instead of improving systems. Do that, and you’ve rebuilt a normal agency, with normal thin margins and normal management headaches. The advantage disappears.

This week. Take one real deliverable, do it by hand, and time every step. Then build a system (AI plus simple automations) that does 80% of it, while you review and finish the last 20%.

Why the economics are so attractive

Consider a bookkeeping practice serving small businesses. A traditional bookkeeper can handle roughly ten to fifteen clients a month. Forty clients at $800 a month brings in $32,000 a month, but it takes three or four staff to serve them. After salaries, software, and overhead, the owner might keep 15 to 25 percent.

Now give one experienced bookkeeper an AI system that categorizes transactions, reconciles accounts, drafts the monthly report, and writes the client update email. The person reviews and handles exceptions. The same forty clients, the same $32,000, but the owner might keep 60 to 75 percent. Figure 1.2 shows the difference.

This creates three advantages at once. You can undercut competitors on price and still earn more per client. You can deliver faster, in twenty-four hours instead of two weeks, which becomes your marketing message. And you can grow without hiring, so every new client adds mostly profit.

Plain English

A traditional firm sells hours, so growing means hiring. An AI-native firm sells results, so growing means improving its systems. Same customers, same price, very different profit.

Choosing Your Niche

The best niche is one you already understand. Run each candidate through five screening questions:

  1. Do I know this work? Have I done it, managed it, or bought it?
  2. Is it repetitive? Is the same type of deliverable produced over and over?
  3. Is it mostly text, documents, or data? That’s where AI is strongest.
  4. Do clients already pay for it? Look for an existing budget line, so you aren’t creating a new habit.
  5. Is it unregulated, or can I partner around the regulation? Law, tax filing, medicine, and financial advice require licenses. You can still work in these fields, but you’ll need a licensed professional who supervises and signs off.

Narrow it down to one service for one type of client. “Monthly bookkeeping for restaurants” is far easier to sell, systematize, and price than “accounting services.” Narrow niches also produce more consistent work, and consistent work is easier to automate.

Phase 1: Do it by hand and time it

Get one real or practice client: a friend’s business, a discounted pilot, or a side project. Do the deliverable start to finish by hand. As you work, fill in a step log like the one below. Record your screen while you work so you can review it later. You’ll be surprised by how many small steps you do without noticing.

Usually 70 to 85 percent of the steps are marked “Yes” or “Partly.” Those are what you automate. The “No” steps (judgment, relationship, and exceptions) are what the client is really paying a professional for, and they’re where your time should go.

Phase 2: Build the system that does 80%

You don’t need to code. You’re connecting existing tools. For each “Yes” step, write a reusable instruction set, a playbook, in your AI assistant’s project or workspace feature, and include examples of excellent past output. Then connect the steps with an automation tool so that, for example, a client’s upload triggers processing, the draft lands in your review queue, and approved work is sent automatically.

Add a human review checkpoint before anything reaches a client. That review is your quality control, and it’s what the client is really paying for. A simple review checklist (numbers tie out, names correct, tone right, open questions listed) catches most AI errors. When you’re done, time the deliverable again. Aim for a five- to tenfold reduction: a six-hour job should take under an hour.

Quality control that scales
  • Confidence flags: ask the AI to mark anything it’s unsure about, and review those items first.
  • Spot checks: review 100% of work for new clients, then a sample once the system proves reliable.
  • Error log: every mistake becomes a new rule in the playbook, so the system gets better every month.
  • Golden examples: keep five excellent past deliverables in the AI’s reference files. Examples teach style better than instructions.

Phase 3: Package and price it

Productize the service: one clear offer with a fixed scope, fixed price, and fixed turnaround, for example, “Monthly books closed by the 5th, with a one-page plain-English report, $750 a month.” A productized offer is easier to sell, easier to automate, and easier to deliver consistently.

Price against the old way, not against your cost. If a traditional firm charges $1,200 and takes three weeks, charge $800 to $1,000 and deliver in three days. Don’t charge $100 just because the work costs you little. Your low cost is your margin and your growth budget, not a discount you owe anyone.

Phase 4: Win your first five to ten clients

  1. Warm network first: former colleagues, past employers, and people in your industry.
  2. Lead lists: use a B2B contact database to find businesses in your niche and their decision-makers.
  3. Outreach: a short, personal email or professional-network message offering a free or discounted first deliverable. Cold-email sending tools can scale this once your message works.
  4. Content: post short before-and-after examples: “Here’s how we closed a restaurant’s books in two hours instead of two weeks.” Simple design and video-editing tools make this easy.
  5. Website: a one-page site with one offer, one price, and one “book a call” button.
  6. Referral partners: businesses that serve the same clients but don’t compete with you. For bookkeeping, that could be tax preparers, business bankers, or point-of-sale vendors.

Phase 5: Scale with systems, not headcount

This is where you avoid the trap. Before every hire, ask: Can I improve the system so I don’t need this person? Track one number above all others, revenue per person. Traditional service firms typically generate roughly $100,000 to $150,000 per employee. Aim for $300,000 to $1,000,000 or more. When you do hire, hire reviewers and operators who manage and improve the AI system, not people to do the work by hand.

Over time, the internal system you’ve built becomes an asset in its own right. You can license it to other firms (Model 2), package your method as a plugin (Model 4), or use it to buy traditional firms and transform their economics, a strategy sometimes called an AI roll-up.

Watch out: the agency trap

Signs you’re sliding back into a traditional agency: revenue per person is flat or falling, you’re hiring to handle volume instead of exceptions, the playbook hasn’t been updated in a month, and turnaround times are getting longer. The fix is always the same. Stop, find the step that’s eating time, and improve the system before adding people.

Case studyA two-person grant-writing studio

The background. A former nonprofit development director knows that small nonprofits pay freelance grant writers $2,000 to $5,000 per application and often wait weeks.

The system. An intake form collects the organization’s programs, budgets, and past proposals. An AI workspace holds a playbook for each major funder type, twenty winning past proposals as examples, and a compliance checklist. The AI drafts the needs statement, program description, and budget narrative; the founder edits for strategy and voice; a part-time reviewer checks every requirement against the funder’s guidelines.

The offer. “A complete, submission-ready grant application in five business days, $1,500.” Monthly retainers for organizations that apply frequently.

The numbers. Time per application falls from about 30 hours to about 6. Two people deliver 25 applications a month (about $37,500) instead of the 6 or 7 a traditional freelancer could manage.

Risks and How to Handle Them

RiskMitigation
AI makes errorsAlways keep a human review step. Use checklists and confidence flags. Never send unreviewed AI output to clients.
Licensing and regulation (law, tax, medical, financial advice)Partner with a licensed professional who supervises and signs off, or stay in the unregulated part of the work.
Client data privacyUse business-tier AI plans that don’t train on your data. Have a confidentiality agreement and data-processing terms ready. Consider a security certification once larger clients ask.
LiabilityForm a limited liability company, buy professional liability (errors and omissions) insurance, and use contracts that define scope clearly.
Turning into an agency (the trap)Review revenue per person monthly. Fix systems before hiring.
Competitors copying youYour moat is niche expertise, client relationships, and refined playbooks, not the AI tools, which everyone can access.

Chapter summary
  • Sell the outcome, not the software. Clients pay for results, not for how you produce them.
  • AI turns a service firm’s biggest cost, labor, into its smallest, which can lift margins from about 20% to about 70%.
  • Pick one service for one client type that you know, that’s repetitive and document-heavy, and that clients already pay for.
  • Do the work by hand first, then automate the 80% and keep a human on the 20% that needs judgment.
  • Price against the old way, deliver faster, and measure revenue per person to avoid becoming an agency.
