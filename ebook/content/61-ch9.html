How to Pick Yours

Don’t pick the model that sounds exciting. Pick the one that matches what you already have.
Learning objectives
  • Read the decision table: advantage needed, revenue, technical lift
  • Answer the three deciding questions about yourself
  • Score all eight models against your own situation
  • Validate your top choice with real people in one week

This is the chapter that turns eight ideas into one decision. It has two parts: a comparison table, and three questions to ask yourself. The key insight is simple: your existing advantage is your head start. You don’t choose a model because it sounds exciting; you choose the one that fits what you already have.

Part 2: The Three Deciding Questions

Question 1: What do you know better than almost anyone?

That’s your niche. You don’t research it from scratch; it’s what you already know from your job, career, hobbies, family life, or community. “Better than almost anyone” doesn’t mean world-class. It means better than the average person, and far better than an engineer who has never done that work.

Question 2: Who already pays for that today, and how?

This points you to the right model by identifying the existing customer:

Table 9.2 • Follow the money that’s already flowing
If people currently pay…Look at…Why
Service fees (firms, consultants, freelancers)Model 1Keep selling the service, delivered ten times more efficiently
For professional toolsModels 2, 3, 4Sell a better tool: a harness, an agent tool, or a plugin
As fans and groups (hobby and group spending)Models 5, 6Build for communities and groups
For data (reports, research, intelligence)Model 7Sell the data

This removes a huge risk. You don’t have to convince anyone to start spending. You redirect money that’s already being spent.

Question 3: Who does the work?

“A person watching a screen points left on the map. An agent in a loop points right.” This is the horizontal axis of the map from the Introduction. On the left, a human does the work with AI as a helper, and products succeed through experience and relationships. On the right, an agent works on its own while humans supervise, and products succeed through reliability, documentation, and scale. Lean left if you’re good with people, design, clients, and community. Lean right if you’re comfortable with systems, automation, and technical building.

Plain English

Question 1 finds your niche. Question 2 finds your customer and their budget. Question 3 finds the kind of product you’ll enjoy building. Where the three answers overlap is your model.

The Decision Process, Step by Step

Step 1: Take inventory of your advantages (days 1–2)

Write honest answers in three groups. What I know: jobs I’ve held and what I did day to day; industries I understand from the inside; problems people ask me for help with; hobbies I could talk about for hours; groups I belong to; methods I’ve used or taught. Who I can reach: past clients, coworkers, and employers; communities where I’m known; any audience I have. My resources: hours per week, money I can invest, how soon I need income, and my technical comfort from 1 (email and spreadsheets) to 5 (I write code).

Try this

Ask an AI assistant: “Interview me one question at a time to inventory my skills, knowledge, networks, and resources for starting a business.” Answering questions is far easier than filling in a blank page. The Workbook’s Advantage Inventory gives you a structured form.

Step 2: Score yourself against all eight models (days 2–3)

Rate each model from 0 to 3 on four criteria: Do I have the required advantage? Can I handle the technical lift? Does it fit my income timeline? Do I want to do this work every day? The two highest totals are your candidates. A blank scorecard is in the Workbook.

Step 3: Apply the three questions to your top two (days 3–4)

For each candidate, complete three sentences: “I know ___ better than almost anyone because ___.” • “Right now, ___ pays ___ to ___ for this.” • “In my product, ___ (a person or an agent) does the work, and the user’s role is ___.” If you can’t fill in the second sentence, that’s a warning sign. Pick a niche where money already flows. To research it, compare prices of existing software on review sites, check what freelancers charge on freelance marketplaces, read complaints about paying for this in forums, and ask an AI assistant to list how businesses in the niche currently pay to solve the problem, with typical prices.

Step 4: Think about stacking (day 4)

The models work best in sequence. If you need income soon, start with Model 1 or Model 4, which make money fastest with the least building, and use that income and learning to fund slower, more defensible models. Chapter 10 shows common paths.

Step 5: Validate before you build (days 5–7)

  1. List twenty potential customers from your network and communities.
  2. Have ten conversations of fifteen to twenty minutes. Ask: How do you handle this today? What does it cost in money and time? What’s most frustrating? If I could ___, would that be worth $___ to you?
  3. Try a simple pre-sale: a one-page site with one offer and a “join the waitlist” or “book a call” button, plus a payment link for deposits.
  4. Decide: three or more people say “yes, I’d pay,” or actually pay a deposit, means go. Lots of “interesting” but no commitments means adjust the offer or test your second choice.

Chapter summary
  • Match the model to the advantage you already have; that’s your head start.
  • Ask three questions: What do I know? Who already pays, and how? Who does the work?
  • Score all eight models; your top two are your candidates.
  • Validate with ten conversations and a pre-sale before building.
  • If you need income soon, start with Model 1 or 4 and stack slower models later.
