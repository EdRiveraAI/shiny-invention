Agent-Native Systems

Teach their agent your method. Give them a window to watch it work.
Learning objectives
  • Explain how a plugin carries a method into a customer’s AI assistant
  • Name the parts of a plugin: skills, commands, sub-agents, and connectors
  • Write a method as three skills: diagnose, plan, do
  • Design a dashboard for watching, not clicking
  • Monetize while protecting yourself from platform risk

Millions of people now spend their workday inside an AI assistant. Each of them effectively has an agent: an AI that can read their files, use their apps, and do tasks for them. This model asks a simple question: if you’re an expert, why sell a course, a coaching call, or a new app, when you could install your know-how straight into the agent your customer already uses?

An agent-native system packages your method so it runs inside the customer’s AI. The agent follows your method, and the customer watches progress and approves important steps on a dashboard. It’s like hiring a consultant whose expertise works inside your own assistant, around the clock.

Key terms
Plugin
An installable package that adds capabilities to an AI assistant: skills, commands, sub-agents, and connectors.
Skill
A written set of instructions, often with templates and examples, that teaches an AI how to do a task your way.
Command
A shortcut the user types to start a workflow, such as /pipeline-review.
Sub-agent
A specialized assistant with a narrow role that the main AI can delegate to.
Connector
A link (often via MCP) that lets the agent use another app, such as email, a calendar, or a CRM.
Platform risk
The danger of depending on a platform you don’t control, whose rules, fees, or features can change.

The Model Explained

The diagram, step by step

  1. Plugin: skills, commands, sub-agents (top box). This is your product: a downloadable package containing your method.
  2. “Your method” arrow. The plugin installs into the customer’s agent and teaches it how you do the work.
  3. Customer → asks → their agent. The customer asks in plain language: “Help me fix my sales pipeline.”
  4. Agent → works → dashboard. The agent does the work using your method and reports progress, results, and decisions to a dashboard.
  5. Dashboard → watch and approve → customer. The customer supervises and approves key decisions without doing the work themselves.

The eight facts, unpacked

Why now. People already work inside AI assistants and AI coding tools all day. Instead of persuading someone to download a new app, you add capability to the tool they already have open. Plugins are the new app store.

Real-world example. Leading AI assistants now support plugins that bundle skills, commands, and sub-agents into one install, and other assistants offer similar ecosystems of custom assistants and app integrations.

Proof it works. The framework’s originator sells their own product-building method as a plugin, paired with a dashboard for watching it run. It’s the same internal “operating system” that powers their service work in Model 1, which shows how the models feed each other.

Who builds it. Experts with a repeatable method: coaches, consultants, and educators. If you’ve taught a framework, run a workshop, or have a “here’s how I do it” process, this is for you. You don’t need to code, because skills are mostly written instructions.

How it earns. Paid plugin access (one-time or yearly), a membership (plugin plus community plus updates), or a dashboard subscription (free plugin, paid dashboard).

The trap. Platform risk. An AI company could change its rules, its marketplace, or its plugin format, or build your feature itself. If your customers exist only inside someone else’s platform, you could lose everything overnight. So own your email list and your community. Those are your real assets.

This week. Write your method as three skills: diagnose (figure out the situation), plan (decide what to do), and do (do it). That’s the shape of almost every consulting method.

A course teaches people what to do. A plugin does it, using what the course would have taught.

Why This Is a Big Opportunity

  • Courses are fading, and this replaces them. People buy courses and never finish them. A plugin doesn’t have to be studied, because it does the work.
  • Coaching doesn’t scale, and this does. You can coach maybe twenty clients. A plugin can serve twenty thousand.
  • Almost no code. Skills are plain-language text files. If you can write a clear process document, you can write a skill.
  • The market is early. Plugin marketplaces are new, and experts who package their method now can own their category.
  • Recurring revenue. Methods evolve, so updates, community, and the dashboard justify a membership.
Case study“Local Growth OS” for a small-business marketing coach
Skill or partWhat it teaches the AI
1. Diagnose (/growth-audit)Review the business’s website, local search listing, reviews, and social accounts. Score them against the coach’s 20-point checklist and identify the top three gaps.
2. Plan (/growth-plan)Turn the gaps into a 30-day plan using the coach’s playbook: what to fix first, which channels, and a weekly content calendar.

Business model: a $49–$99 monthly membership (plugin, updates, dashboard, private community), plus a higher tier with group coaching calls.

Phase 0: Define your method

  1. Write it down as if training a new employee: your process start to finish, your frameworks and checklists, and what separates a good result from a bad one.
  2. Collect your examples: best past deliverables, templates, scripts, and before-and-after cases. Examples are what make an AI follow your style instead of giving generic advice.
  3. Split it into diagnose, plan, and do.
  4. Pick a narrow audience. “Real-estate agents growing their listings” beats “anyone who wants to grow.”
Try this

Record yourself explaining your method, or coaching a real client, using an AI meeting recorder. Then ask an AI assistant to turn the transcript into numbered steps, checklists, and rules. It’s much faster than writing from a blank page.

Phase 1: Write your three skills

A skill is usually a folder containing a SKILL.md file: a short description, so the AI knows when to use it, followed by your instructions. A simplified example:

---
name: growth-audit
description: Audit a local business's online presence using the
  Local Growth 20-point checklist. Use when the user asks for a
  marketing audit, a diagnosis, or "what should I fix first."
---

# Local Growth Audit

## Step 1: Gather
Ask for: business name, website, local listing link, top 2 competitors.

## Step 2: Score against the checklist
Score each item 0-2 (see checklist.md). Never guess; mark "unknown"
if you can't verify.

## Step 3: Report
Output: overall score out of 40, the top 3 gaps ranked by impact,
and one quick win doable today. Use the tone in voice.md: direct,
encouraging, no jargon.

Put supporting files in the same folder, such as checklist.md, voice.md, example reports, and templates, and the AI reads them when needed. Write and test skills in a paid AI assistant (some include a built-in “skill creator” to help you draft), then install and test the plugin in the assistant’s desktop or coding app. Run each skill on five to ten real scenarios and compare the output with what you would produce. Edit until it reaches 80 to 90 percent of your quality.

Phase 2: Package it as a plugin

A typical plugin is a folder you host in a code repository:

local-growth-os/
├── .plugin/plugin.json        ← name, version, description, author
├── skills/
│   ├── growth-audit/SKILL.md
│   ├── growth-plan/SKILL.md
│   └── growth-week/SKILL.md
├── commands/                  ← slash commands like /growth-audit
├── agents/
│   └── review-responder.md    ← sub-agent definitions
└── connectors.json            ← optional: links to email, CRM, dashboard

Exact folder and file names vary by AI platform, so follow your platform’s plugin documentation. To distribute, many platforms let you publish a marketplace, which is simply a repository listing your plugins. Customers add your marketplace and install with one command. Use a public repository for a free version and a private one for paid access.

Design rule: the dashboard shows status, results, and decisions waiting for approval. It shouldn’t be a complicated tool full of buttons. The agent does the work; the human watches and says yes or no.

Pricing ideas: free for the diagnose skill alone; $29–$99 a month for the full plugin, dashboard, updates, and community; $300–$2,000 a month for a done-with-you tier with live coaching (a blend with Model 1); and team licenses for companies rolling it out to staff.

Phase 5: Own your audience from day one

This is the antidote to platform risk. Every customer should be on your list, not merely installed on someone else’s platform. Use an email newsletter platform, a community platform, a simple website, and a free CRM. Require an email address for the free skill. Announce updates by email (“New in v1.3: /holiday-campaign”). Build versions for more than one AI platform, so no single company controls your business. Keep your method documents in your own files, because the plugin is only one way of packaging them.

Phase 6: Launch and grow

  1. Your existing audience first: past clients, students, subscribers, followers.
  2. Demo videos: the customer types one command, the agent works, the dashboard fills in. Show “watching, not clicking.”
  3. Plugin directories and marketplaces, plus a clear README in your repository.
  4. Partnerships with associations, software vendors, and other coaches serving the same audience.
  5. Webinars and workshops: teach the method live, then offer the plugin that does it.
  6. Monthly iteration: dashboard data shows where people get stuck and which drafts get rejected. Improve the skills and add new ones members request.

Risks and How to Handle Them

RiskMitigation
Platform risk (the trap)Own the email list and community, support multiple AI platforms, and keep your method independent of any one format.
People copying your skillsInstalled skill files can be read. The real value is ongoing updates, the dashboard, the community, and you. Keep premium assets (data, templates, the dashboard) on your own servers.
Bad AI output harming your reputationApproval steps, testing on real cases, “never do” rules, and showing sources and reasoning on the dashboard.
Customer data privacyKeep data in the customer’s own tools where possible, publish a clear privacy policy, and collect only what the dashboard needs.
Customers not using itAn onboarding email sequence, a five-minute first win (the diagnose skill), and weekly nudges.
Method too vagueIf you can’t write it as clear steps and checklists, refine it with real clients first.

Chapter summary
  • Package your method as a plugin that installs into the customer’s AI. Their agent does the work your way.
  • A plugin combines skills, commands, sub-agents, and optional connectors, mostly written in plain language.
  • Structure almost any method as diagnose, plan, do.
  • The dashboard exists so customers can watch and approve, not operate software.
  • Defend against platform risk by owning your email list and community and supporting multiple platforms.
